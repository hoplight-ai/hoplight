Where Political Money Actually Works

Federal election spending has tripled in real dollars since 2000. But the cost of winning a competitive state legislative seat remains a fraction of a competitive federal one, and state power delivers the policy outcomes that matter most to working people. The question for labor leaders is not whether to spend, but where a dollar does more.

These ratios have held steady across five midterm cycles

Both federal and state costs have risen 5-10x since 2006. The purchasing power gap has been roughly constant. One Senate seat has always cost the equivalent of 270-390 state house campaigns. This is not a new problem. The answer has been available for 16 years.

2010: The cost of not investing in state power
Labor lost 20 state legislative chambers. Wisconsin passed Act 10, gutting public-sector collective bargaining. Right-to-work laws spread across MI, OH, IN. Voter ID proliferated. Republicans took control of redistricting that shaped the next decade. Total cost to have defended key state chambers: a fraction of what was spent on federal races that year.
2022: What state investment delivered
Michigan repealed right-to-work and codified abortion rights. Minnesota passed free school meals, paid family leave, and voting rights restoration. Pennsylvania broke the GOP supermajority. Four chambers flipped to Democrats. Meanwhile, the Georgia Senate race consumed $425M. Warnock won. Name the federal legislation it produced.

What state power actually delivers for workers

State legislatures set minimum wage, public sector collective bargaining rights, right-to-work law, Medicaid expansion, paid leave mandates, workplace safety enforcement, and redistricting rules. A single state trifecta can pass more pro-worker legislation in one session than a federal majority has managed in a decade. The US House, even with a Democratic majority, cannot pass legislation past the Senate filibuster, cannot hold officials accountable through conviction, and cannot deliver policy outcomes that reach workers' lives. The federal investment buys representation. The state investment buys power.

Reaching voters costs more and works less than it used to

36% → 6%
Political phone contact rate since 1997. Pew's telephone survey response rate, which tracks political contact specifically, collapsed from 36% to 6% by 2018. Campaign live-call answer rates hit 6.5% per dial in 2024. You now dial 15 numbers to reach one voter. In 1997, you needed 3. Pew abandoned phone polling entirely after 2019.
$1.50 → $3.00
Cost per door knock (vendor/paid canvassing per contact attempt). Driven by canvasser wages rising from ~$9/hr to ~$22/hr while federal minimum wage has been frozen at $7.25 since 2009. This is what you pay per door, not per vote.
$19 → $40
Cost per incremental vote from door knocking. A separate metric: Green and Gerber's research shows it takes ~14 contacts to produce 1 additional vote. Cost per vote = 14 contacts × cost per contact hour. As wages rise, this rises too.

Cost per knock and cost per vote are different line items. Cost per knock ($3) is what you pay per door contact attempt. Cost per incremental vote ($40) is what you pay to produce one additional vote through sustained canvassing. Phone banking, once cost-competitive with knocking, now runs $35-60+ per vote due to collapsing contact rates. Campaign phone contact rate sources: Pew Research Center methodological reports (1997-2019), CampaignNow 2024 operational data (6.5% live answer rate). All canvassing figures are for paid/vendor programs, not volunteer.
